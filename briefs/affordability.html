Affordability

How it is measured and how it has changed since 2021

November 2026

* Prepared in a personal capacity; the views are the author’s own and do not represent Rice University.

Executive Summary

This brief asks whether everyday life has become less affordable for American households since 2021, and for whom. It measures affordability in three ways (real income, the burden of essential costs and the cost of starting a household) and uses the results to identify where federal policy could do the most.

  • Three measures of affordability. 1. Real income measures whether nominal income keeps up with prices, on average and across the distribution. 2. The cost burden measures how many households struggle to cover essential purchases, such as housing and food. 3. Entry costs measure how expensive it is to start a household (e.g. costs of purchasing a first home or car, and the costs of having a child) (Section 1).

  • On average, nominal income has kept up with prices since 2021, but the poorest households have lost ground relative to the rest since 2015. From 2021 to 2025, consumer prices rose 18.8% and average hourly pay 19.0%, and real median household income is now above its pre-pandemic peak. Prices for essentials such as housing, home energy and food rose faster than prices overall and take a larger share of the budgets of low-income households. Since 2015, real income at the 10th percentile has grown about a third as fast as at the median (Section 2).

  • Progress on cost burdens stalled after 2019, mostly because of food insecurity and housing. Cost burdens fell steadily from 2015 to 2019, but by 2024 more than a third of that improvement had been undone. Food insecurity rose from a record low of 10.2% in 2021 to 13.7% in 2024, a decade high. Housing burdens rose mainly for households that move or buy at today's prices. Health premiums kept pace with incomes, while child care and the running costs of a car outpaced them (Section 3).

  • Starting a household has become much more expensive, mostly because of housing. The mortgage payment on a typical first home rose from about a quarter of a young renter's income in 2021 to over 40% in 2024, mainly because mortgage rates more than doubled. A third fewer young households bought a home in 2024 than in 2021. A young family starting out now needs two-thirds of a young household's income for essentials, and three-quarters if it buys (Section 4).

  • In Texas, the lowest incomes have lagged further, the state's housing-cost advantage is gone, and food insecurity and gaps in health coverage are well above national rates. Real income at the 10th percentile fell 1.3% from 2015 to 2024 while it rose 3.7% nationally. The share of Texas households spending over 30% of income on housing rose from 30.6% to 34.0% and now exceeds the national share, with property taxes and home insurance adding to owners' costs. 17.6% of Texas households are food insecure and 16.7% of Texans are uninsured, against 13.3% and 8.2% nationally (Section 5).

  • Based on these measures of affordability, the largest gains from policy are in addressing housing supply, the social safety net and food security, and health care coverage. Senator-elect Talarico's proposals to expand the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit and ease construction, expand the Child Tax Credit and Earned Income Tax Credit while protecting SNAP, and reinstate the enhanced ACA premium credits map onto these priorities. Universal child care addresses a smaller but fast-rising cost. A gas-tax suspension offers visible but small relief (Section 6).

  • Measured spending and income may understate the strain. Realized expenditures and income may not fully reflect what households pay (financially or psychologically) to keep their lifestyle at a given level, such as drawing down savings, borrowing, working longer hours or searching for a higher-paying job, trading down in quality of goods and services, or delaying moving out, buying a home or having children.

1. How do economists measure "affordability"?

Affordability is not captured by a single statistic. In this note, I use three measures of the purchasing power of household income: i) real income, ii) the cost burden for essential purchases, and iii) the cost of starting a household.

2. Real income: has pay kept up with prices?

Summary. On average, household incomes have kept up with prices since 2021, and real median household income is now above its pre-pandemic peak. But the prices of essentials such as housing, home energy and food have risen faster than prices overall, and these take a larger share of lower- and middle-income budgets. Across the distribution, real income growth since 2021 has been strongest in the lower-middle and weakest at the top. However, over the longer run since 2015, the poorest households have fallen behind, especially once their costlier basket of essentials is counted.

Average purchasing power has increased since 2021

From 2021 to 2025, consumer prices rose 18.8%, while average hourly pay rose 19.0% for all private-sector workers and 21.0% for production and nonsupervisory workers. In January–September 2026, prices averaged 22.5% above their 2021 level, and pay 22.5% and 24.6% above for private-sector and production and nonsupervisory workers, respectively.

Real median household income, in 2025 dollars, was $83,270 in 2021. It fell to $81,460 in 2022, when inflation peaked, and rose to $87,460 in 2025: 5% above its 2021 level and 2.5% above its pre-pandemic peak of $85,320 in 2019. Mean household income rose by a similar 5.2%, from $120,400 to $126,700. Real median weekly earnings of full-time workers rose 1.9% from 2021 to 2025.

Prices of essential expenditures have outpaced average incomes

Home energy and utilities, housing costs (tenants' rent and owners' equivalent rent), and restaurant meals rose faster than nonsupervisory pay and faster than average prices. Groceries rose slightly less than pay but faster than overall prices. Gasoline jumped in 2026 and is now 27% above its 2021 average, while used cars are slightly cheaper than in 2021.

Measured medical-care prices rose only 13%, but this understates what households pay for health coverage, which the CPI measures only indirectly (Section 3). Child care is similar. It carries little weight in the CPI because most households pay nothing for it, but for families who do, the average price rose 29% from 2020 to 2024, against 22% for overall prices (Section 3).

The fastest-rising costs weigh most on lower- and middle-income households

The categories whose prices have risen fastest since 2021 (shelter, home energy, groceries, restaurant meals and, in 2026, gasoline) take a much larger share of lower- and middle-income budgets. In 2021 they made up 53% of spending for the lowest fifth of households, 45% for the middle fifth and 36% for the highest fifth.

Real income growth lags for the bottom 10% since 2015 and for above-median households since 2021

Since 2015, median real income has risen 1.2% a year (1.1% using the basket of goods consumed by households at the median). Real income of the poorest 10% of households, however, rose only 0.4% a year since 2015. Controlling for their basket of goods, real incomes rose even more slowly, at 0.3% a year because shelter and utilities, which take a larger share of the poorest budgets, rose faster than other prices.

From 2021 to 2024, real household income rose most in the lower-middle of the distribution: about 1% a year between the 15th and 30th percentiles, against 0.6% a year at the 10th, 0.5% at the median and 0.2% at the 90th, using each group's own index (with the CPI: about 1%, 0.7%, 0.4% and 0.2%). This is consistent with the strong wage gains of low-wage workers in 2021–23.

3. Cost burden: can a household cover the essentials?

Measuring the cost burden asks whether a household can cover essential purchases out of its income. While the previous section compared pay with prices on average, the cost burden looks at how much of income goes to housing (including utilities), food, child care, health care and transportation.

A household is counted as cost-burdened in a category when its spending there exceeds a set share of its gross income: 30% for housing (above 50% is severe), 7% for child care, 10% for out-of-pocket health costs, 6% for home energy and 15% for transportation. The measures below report the fraction of households that are cost-burdened in each category and how that number has changed since 2021.

Summary. Cost burdens across essentials fell steadily before the pandemic, but that progress has stalled since, with food insecurity and housing accounting for the reversal. Housing burdens have risen mainly for households that move or buy at today's prices, while long-time renters and owners have seen little change. Health premiums have kept pace with incomes, but child care and the running costs of a car have outpaced them. Food insecurity has reached a decade high even though food takes a smaller share of lower-income budgets.

Housing: more households are burdened by housing costs, and the share of owners burdened has risen since 2021

The share of renter households that are cost-burdened held at 49% from 2021 to 2024, while the share of owner households rose from about 23% to 24%. The number of cost-burdened households rose for both, because there are more households (JCHS 2026):

  • Renters. A record 22.7 million renter households (49%) spent more than 30% of income on housing in 2024, 1.1 million more than in 2021, when the share was also 49%: the rise reflects more renter households, not a higher share. Of those, 12.1 million (26%) spent more than half their income, up from 11.6 million (also 26%) in 2021. Among renters earning under $30,000, 83% spent more than 30% of income on housing.

  • Owners. Owners spending more than 30% of income on housing rose by 1.7 million from 2021, to 20.7 million in 2024, or from about 23% to 24% of all owner households; the share spending more than half rose from about 10% to 11% (ACS microdata).

  • Rents and insurance. CPI rent rose 24.6% from 2021 to 2025, against 18.8% for all prices. Average home insurance premiums on mortgaged homes rose 64% from the end of 2021 to the end of 2025 (Section 4).

  • Home energy. Among households that pay for energy directly, 24.6% spent more than 6% of income on it in 2024 and 14.0% spent more than 10%, little changed from 2021 (24.5% and 13.6%) after a rise in 2022–23. The median household energy bill rose from $2,160 to $2,520 a year (ACS microdata).

New versus existing households. The rise in housing burdens falls on households that move or buy now, at today's prices. Long-time renters and owners, many with low locked-in mortgage rates or rents that reset slowly, saw little change (ACS microdata):

Health costs kept pace with incomes, but child-care prices outran them

  • Health coverage. The average employer family premium reached $26,993 in 2025, up 21.5% from $22,221 in 2021; workers paid $6,850 of it directly, up 14.8% (KFF 2025). Premiums roughly kept pace with incomes: from 2021 to 2025, median household income rose 23.6% and average hourly earnings 19.0%, so the worker's share fell from 8.4% to 7.8% of median household income (Census H-5; BLS). The enhanced ACA marketplace premium credits expired after 2025, so marketplace enrollees face steeper net premiums.

  • Out-of-pocket burden. Using the Commonwealth Fund's thresholds, 10.4% of families spent at least 10% of income (5% if below twice the poverty line) on out-of-pocket medical costs other than premiums in 2025, down from 12.1% in 2021. Counting premiums, 16.5% spent at least 10% of income, down from 18.5% (CPS ASEC microdata).

  • Buying your own coverage. Households whose head, under 65, buys coverage directly (about 9% of such households) are about twice as likely as those with employer coverage to spend 10% or more of income on premiums and out-of-pocket costs: 28.6% against 14.0% in 2024, and 31.4% against 15.5% in 2021 (CPS ASEC microdata). The enhanced marketplace tax credits held these burdens down through 2025. With their expiry, premium payments for subsidized enrollees keeping the same plan were estimated to rise 114% in 2026, and a KFF survey found 9% of 2025 marketplace enrollees had become uninsured (KFF 2026).

  • Child care. Child Care Aware of America puts the national average price of care at $13,128 in 2024, up 29% since 2020, when overall prices rose 22% and average household incomes rose 24% (Census H-5). That is about 10% of a married couple's median income and 35% of a single parent's (The 74). The federal benchmark for affordable care is 7% (DOL 2023).

  • Share above the child-care benchmark. Among families with a child under 13, about 24% paid for child care in both 2021 and 2025, and 38% of those paid more than 7% of income in both years, or about 9% of all families with young children. The median amount paid rose from $5,400 to $7,200 (CPS ASEC microdata, work-related child care).

  • Families with very young children. About 16% of households whose youngest child is under 5 spent more than 7% of income on child care in 2024, against about 4% of households whose youngest is 5 to 12; both shares have changed little since 2015. Among families with a child under 5 who pay for care, the median amount paid rose 23%, from $7,200 in 2021 to $8,840 in 2024 (CPS ASEC microdata).

Food insecurity is at a decade high, even though food takes a smaller share of the poorest households' income than in 2021

There is no standard cost-burden cutoff for food, so we report its share of income and the rate of food insecurity.

  • Food insecurity. The share of households that were food insecure at some point in the year rose from a record low of 10.2% in 2021, when pandemic-era aid such as the expanded Child Tax Credit was in place, to 13.7% in 2024, the highest since 2014 (USDA ERS, via Agri-Pulse). At the bottom, the spending share fell while food insecurity rose: spending shares miss households that cut back. The 2024 report is the last, because USDA has ended the survey.

  • Spending share. Food took 33.0% of before-tax income for the lowest-income fifth of households in 2024, 12.2% for the middle fifth and 6.4% for the highest (ERS 2026). In 2021 the shares were 37.0%, 12.0% and 6.2% (BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey, via FRED). Grocery prices rose faster than prices overall, 21.0% against 18.8% from 2021 to 2025, and restaurant prices rose 24.4%, but reported incomes at the bottom rose faster still.

Car running costs surged, but transportation's share of lower incomes fell

The Department of Transportation treats spending more than 15% of income on transportation as cost-burdened (DOT 2023). Published tables report averages by income group rather than the share of households above that line, so we compare each group's average with it.

  • Spending share. Transportation took 30.6% of before-tax income for the lowest-income fifth of households in 2024, 19.6% for the second fifth and 15.7% for the middle fifth, so on average the bottom three fifths are above DOT's line. In 2021 the shares were 32.5%, 23.0% and 16.8% (BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey, via FRED). About 30% of the lowest-income households have no vehicle at all (BTS 2024).

  • Cost of keeping a car. The running costs of a car rose much faster than prices overall. Motor vehicle insurance rose 57% from December 2021 to December 2025, against 16% for all prices (BLS 2026), and repair and maintenance prices rose 35.7% from 2021 to 2025. Vehicle prices rose less: 14.2% for new vehicles and 1.3% for used ones. Gasoline in 2025 was only 5.7% above its 2021 average, but from January to September 2026 it averaged 20% more than a year earlier. Insurance prices have begun to ease in 2026 (BLS CPI, June 2026).

Overall, progress on cost burdens stalled after 2019; since 2021 they are up about 4%, mostly from food insecurity

To summarize the extent to which households are cost burdened, the cost-burden index counts the average number of essentials on which a household is cost-burdened. It adds up the share of all households over each threshold, across housing, home energy, out-of-pocket health costs, child care and food insecurity¹. A value of 0.80 means 80 burdens per 100 households. The series starts in 2015 for comparison, and 2020 is omitted because pandemic disruptions to data collection make that year's surveys unreliable.

The index fell from 0.89 burdens per household in 2015 to 0.76 in 2019, as every category declined, led by home energy and out-of-pocket health costs. That progress stopped after the pandemic. By 2024 the index was back up to 0.81, 6% above its 2019 low, undoing more than a third of the 2015–19 improvement. Food insecurity (3.2 more burdens per 100 households) and housing (2.5 more) account for the rise since 2019, while out-of-pocket health burdens kept falling. Since 2021 alone, the index rose about 4%, almost all from food insecurity.

¹ Transportation is left out because no large survey measures it household by household.

4. Entry costs: how easy is it to start a household?

Another way to characterize affordability is to measure the costs of starting a household, including the cost of major first purchases, like a home or car, and the costs of having a first child.

Summary. Buying a first home has become much more expensive since 2021, mainly because of higher mortgage rates and, to a lesser extent, higher home prices and insurance premiums. Young households have responded by buying less often rather than buying smaller homes. Housing costs have also led the rise in the overall cost of starting a family, especially for families that buy rather than rent.

The cost of purchasing a first home has increased since 2021, and a third fewer young households are buying

For the typical first-time buyer, the cost of a first home has risen much faster than for the median household. Take the median value of homes bought in the past year by householders aged 25–44: $320,000 in 2021 and $400,000 in 2024. With 10% down at that year's average 30-year rate, the payment rose from 26.5% of the median income of renters aged 25–44 in 2021 to 41.9% in 2024, after peaking at 44.6% in 2023. Young households who did buy had far higher incomes than young renters ($126,900 against $66,600 in 2024). Their actual mortgage payments rose from 17.5% to 22.0% of income, and their number fell by a third, from 2.75 million to 1.85 million a year (Census ACS microdata).

Higher costs show up mainly in whether young households buy at all. In 2024, 4.2 of every 100 households headed by someone aged 25–44 bought a home with a mortgage, down from 6.5 in 2021 and lower than in any year since at least 2015.

Conditional on buying, there is modest evidence of downgrading. Compared with 2021, young buyers' homes have about as many bedrooms, and they cost slightly more relative to the typical owner-occupied home in the same area, even comparing buyers with the same income. Young buyers have moved toward lower-priced parts of the country; slightly fewer buy detached single-family homes (81.5% against 82.8%), and slightly more buy mobile homes (3.0% against 2.0%) (Census ACS microdata).

Mortgage rates and home insurance costs have increased since 2021

Mortgage rates. The 30-year fixed rate averaged 3.0% in 2021, jumped to 5.3% in 2022, and has stayed between 6.5% and 6.8% each year since. It was 7.18% on November 5, 2026 (Freddie Mac, via FRED). The swing is large for anyone buying now: at the same price, going from 3.0% in 2021 to 6.6% in 2025 raises monthly principal and interest by about 52%, from $419 to $639 per $100,000 borrowed.

About three-quarters of the rise in mortgage rates came from a rise in risk-free rates, measured by Treasury yields: the 10-year yield rose from 1.5% in 2021 to 4.3% in 2025. The spread between mortgage rates and Treasury yields also widened, from 1.5 to 2.3 points. It peaked at 2.9 points in 2023, well above its 2000–2019 average of 1.8, and has narrowed to about 2.0 in 2026 (Freddie Mac; Treasury, via FRED).

The wider spread does not reflect more borrowers defaulting. Most mortgages are guaranteed by Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac or FHA, and the share of mortgage balances newly 90 or more days delinquent, 1.3% in 2025, was close to its 2019 level and well below its 2003–2019 average of 2.9% (New York Fed 2026). It likely reflects higher interest-rate volatility, which raises the value of borrowers' option to prepay, and the Fed's exit from buying mortgage bonds (Frame and McCormick 2023).

Home insurance. Insurance premiums have also risen, affecting both new buyers and current owners. In Newrez's data on about 1.2 million escrowed mortgages, the average premium rose 64%, from $1,597 at the end of 2021 to $2,625 at the end of 2025, compared with 17% from 2016 to 2020 (Newrez 2026). Growth slowed to 10% in 2025. Texas's average, $3,952, was the third-highest of any state, after Louisiana and Florida.

A young family starting out spends two-thirds of its income on essentials, and three-quarters if it buys a home

To capture entry costs across the essentials, this index prices a starting-household budget each year: what a family of two adults and a child under 5 would pay to set up now. It adds the rent on a new lease for a unit with two or more bedrooms (or, if buying, the mortgage payment on a typical recently bought home), child care for a child under 5, the worker's share of an employer family health premium, and food and transportation for a household headed by someone aged 25–34, at each year's prices. It divides the total by the median income of households headed by someone aged 25–34.

For a renting family, the budget took 66% of a young household's median income in 2024, the same as in 2021 and up from 64% in 2019. Costs rose 20% from 2021 to 2024, led by rent on new leases (25%) and child care (23%), but the median income of young households rose just as fast.

Buying instead of renting changes the picture: the mortgage payment on a typical recently bought home roughly doubled, and the budget rose from 62% of income in 2021 to 74% in 2024. Two caveats: the income of young householders may overstate how young adults are doing, because those who cannot afford to set up a household are not counted; and a two-adult family will often earn more than the median young household.

5. Affordability in Texas

Summary. Texas households face the same pressures as households nationally, with three differences.

  • Since 2015, real income growth in Texas has lagged behind the US and is negative for the poorest households.

  • The state's housing-cost advantage has disappeared.

  • Food insecurity and gaps in health coverage are much more common in Texas than in the US.

Incomes at the bottom have fallen behind. The chart below compares real household income growth in Texas and the nation across the income distribution. From 2015 to 2024 (left panel), Texas incomes grew more slowly at every percentile, and at the 10th percentile they fell 1.9% while the nation's rose 2.3% over the time period. Since 2021 (right panel), Texas incomes have grown faster than the nation's from the 25th percentile up, but the 10th percentile still trails, up 0.3% in Texas against 1.7% nationally.

Texas has lost its housing-cost advantage. In 2015, 30.6% of Texas households spent more than 30% of their income on housing, below the national 32.8%. By 2024 the Texas share was 34.0% and the national share 32.6%. The cost burdened share of households rose from 47.3% to 52.0% among Texas renters (US: 50.1% to 51.0%) and from 20.5% to 23.6% among Texas owners (US: 23.3% in both years). For owners, much of the pressure comes from costs other than the mortgage; the median Texas homeowner paid $4,110 in property tax and $2,230 for home insurance in 2024, against $3,100 and $1,520 nationally, and insurance bills rose 45% from 2021 to 2024 in Texas against 34% nationally.

Food insecurity and health coverage are the widest gaps. Over 2022–2024, 17.6% of Texas households were food insecure, against 13.3% nationally (USDA ERS 2025). In 2024, 16.7% of Texans had no health insurance, the highest rate of any state and about twice the national 8.2% (SHADAC 2025).

6. Where can policy be most effective?

Sections 2–4 point to three findings. First, the poorest households have lost ground relative to the rest of the distribution since 2015. Second, cost burdens have risen most for food and housing since 2019. Third, the higher cost of starting a household comes mostly from housing: mortgage payments, new-lease rents and fewer young households able to buy. In addition, the expiration of the enhanced ACA premium credits after 2025 adds a new cost shock for households that buy their own coverage. Two areas matter less by these measures. Transportation's share of lower-income budgets fell from 2021 to 2024, although car insurance and repair costs rose sharply and gasoline (a highly visible price that shapes how many households judge affordability) jumped in 2026. His proposal to suspend the federal gas and diesel taxes until prices fall back would give visible relief on this salient price, about $200 a year for a typical driver by his campaign's estimate, but it is small next to the rise in housing, food and health costs. Home-energy burdens are little changed from 2021. Proposals in these areas, such as requiring data centers to pay for their own grid upgrades, address future risks more than current sources of strain.

Data and methods

  • Prices. CPI-U series from FRED (seasonally adjusted where available): CPIAUCSL, CUSR0000SAH1 (shelter), CUSR0000SAH2 (fuels and utilities), CUSR0000SEHA (rent), CUSR0000SEHC (owners' equivalent rent), CUSR0000SAF11, CUSR0000SEFV, CUSR0000SEHF01, CUSR0000SEHF02, CUSR0000SETA01, CUSR0000SETA02, CUSR0000SETB01, CUSR0000SETD (vehicle maintenance and repair), CPIMEDSL, CPIAPPSL. Changes compare annual averages.

  • Pay and income. AHETPI (average hourly earnings, production and nonsupervisory), MEHOINUSA672N (real median household income, 2025 dollars), MEHOINUSA646N (nominal median household income).

  • Payment-to-income. The monthly payment on price P with a 20% down payment and monthly rate i (the annual-average 30-year fixed rate, MORTGAGE30US, divided by 12) is

The ratio is 12m divided by median household income. P is the annual-average median sales price of new houses (MSPUS). This is the median-new-home benchmark in the Section 4 chart; the first-time buyer payment (below) uses 10% down instead.

  • Budget-weighted inflation. Each fifth's 2021 shares for food at home, food away, shelter, utilities (priced with the fuels-and-utilities index), vehicles (average of new and used), gasoline, medical care and apparel. The remaining share is priced at the all-items CPI. Result for 2021–2025: 19.3% (lowest fifth), 18.6% (each of the other fifths). For 2021 to January–September 2026: 23.3% (lowest), 22.6% (second and middle), 22.5% (fourth), 22.3% (highest). The fast-rising share sums each fifth's 2021 shares for shelter, utilities, food at home, food away from home and gasoline, the categories whose CPI rose faster than the all-items index over 2021 to January–September 2026. For the ACS income comparisons in Section 2, the same index is built with 2021 shares for 2021–2024 (16.1% for the lowest fifth, 15.5–15.6% for the others) and with 2015 shares for 2015–2024 (34.0% for the lowest fifth, 33.0–33.3% for the others).

  • Mortgage rates. MORTGAGE30US (Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey). The payment change per $100,000 borrowed applies the payment formula above to the loan amount. Treasury yield: GS10 (10-year, annual average); the mortgage spread is the 30-year rate minus GS10. Mortgage delinquency: share of mortgage balances newly 90 or more days delinquent, New York Fed Household Debt and Credit Report data file (2026 Q3).

  • Income by percentile. ACS 1-year public-use household files (PUMS) for 2015, 2021 and 2024: households only (group quarters excluded), about 1.23, 1.28 and 1.35 million household records. Income is total household income before taxes (HINCP), in current dollars using the ACS adjustment factor (ADJINC), and percentiles are weighted by the household weight (WGTP). Each income is spread evenly over ±2.5% of its value before percentiles are taken, to smooth bunching at round numbers. Real growth deflates by the CPI-U (+15.8% for 2021–2024 and +32.4% for 2015–2024, annual averages) or by the price index of the income fifth the percentile falls in, built from 2021 CEX budget shares for 2021–2024 and 2015 shares for 2015–2024 (Budget-weighted inflation, above). The chart shows compound average annual rates, (1 + total real growth)^(1/years) − 1, so the three- and nine-year windows are comparable. The unsmoothed medians are within 1% of Census's published ACS medians. The CPS comparison in the chart note uses the CPS ASEC public-use household files for 2022 and 2026 (income years 2021 and 2025, about 59,000 and 53,000 households), built the same way, with the CPI-U up 18.8% over 2021–2025.

  • Food and transportation shares. BLS Consumer Expenditure Survey means by fifth of income before taxes, 2021 and 2024, via FRED (series CXUFOODTOTLLB0102M–06M, CXUTRANSLB0102M–06M and CXUINCBEFTXLB0102M–06M): average spending divided by average income before taxes. The lowest fifth reports spending well above its income ($30,869 against $13,165 in 2021), largely because income is underreported in the survey, so shares for that group overstate the burden; the change from 2021 to 2024 is more reliable than the level. Price changes are CPI-U annual averages via FRED, except motor vehicle insurance, which is December to December from BLS.

  • Owner cost-burden shares. JCHS counts of cost-burdened owner households (19.0 million in 2021, 20.7 million in 2024) divided by owner-occupied households in the ACS 1-year files (83.5 million and 86.7 million). The 2024 result, 23.9%, matches JCHS's published 24%.

  • New versus existing households. ACS 1-year files for 2019, 2021 and 2024. Households that moved in within the past 12 months (MV = 1) are compared with those in their home two years or more (MV of 3 or higher). Owners who bought are mortgaged owners (TEN = 1); long-time owners also include those who own free and clear. Burdened means housing costs over 30% of income (OCPIP for owners, GRPIP for renters), with zero or negative income counted as burdened.

  • Texas comparisons. ACS 1-year household files for 2015, 2021 and 2024, with Texas households identified by state of residence (about 97,000, 98,000 and 110,000 records). Income percentiles are unsmoothed and deflated by the national CPI-U, because there is no statewide price index; using BEA state and metro price levels (BEA 2025) and income per person, the Texas shortfall at the 10th percentile over 2015–2024 is 3.7 to 4.9 points. The Section 5 chart uses smoothed percentiles and each income fifth's own price index, as in Section 2. Housing burdens use the definition under "New versus existing households" and cover all renters paying cash rent and all owners. Property tax and home insurance are medians among owners reporting a positive amount. The first-home payment and the home-buyer rate follow the Section 4 definitions (householders aged 25–44; a 10% down payment at the year's average 30-year rate). State food insecurity is USDA's 2022–2024 average; the uninsured rate is the ACS estimate for all ages.

  • Health, child-care and home-energy burden shares. Health and child care use the CPS ASEC public-use files for 2022 and 2026 (income years 2021 and 2025). Health: families (official poverty definition) whose out-of-pocket medical spending other than premiums (PMED_VAL plus POTC_VAL, summed over members) is at least 10% of family income, or 5% if income is below twice the poverty threshold; the variant adds premiums (PHIP_VAL) with a 10% cutoff. Child care: SPM units with a child under 13; spending is the unit's work-related child-care expense, which the Census Bureau caps at the lower earner's earnings, over the unit's cash income. Home energy uses the ACS 1-year files for 2021 and 2024: annual electricity, gas and other fuel costs paid directly (ELEP, GASP, FULP) over household income (HINCP times ADJINC), excluding households whose energy is included in rent. In all three, households with zero or negative income and positive spending count as burdened.

  • Health burden by coverage type and child care for young children. CPS ASEC public-use files. Coverage (income years 2021 and 2024): households whose reference person is under 65, grouped by that person's coverage (employer or other group plan; direct purchase without group coverage; uninsured); burdened means premiums plus out-of-pocket medical costs of at least 10% of household income. Young children (income years 2015–2019 and 2021–2024): households whose youngest child is under 5, or 5 to 12, with child-care spending (CARE_VAL, later HCHCARE_VAL) over 7% of household income; the median amount paid is among households that pay for care.

  • Cost-burden index. The sum of five shares of all households in each year from 2015 to 2019 and 2021 to 2024; 2020 is omitted because pandemic disruptions to data collection affected the ACS and the CPS. Housing: costs over 30% of income (ACS 1-year files; owners' OCPIP or renters' GRPIP above 30, with zero or negative income counted as burdened and renters paying no cash rent counted as not burdened). Home energy: annual electricity, gas and other fuel costs paid directly, over 6% of household income (ACS). Health: out-of-pocket medical costs other than premiums at least 10% of household income, or 5% if income is below twice the poverty threshold of the householder's family (CPS ASEC). Child care: household spending on child care (CARE_VAL, later HCHCARE_VAL) over 7% of household income (CPS ASEC). The CPS files are for 2016–2020 and 2022–2025, covering income in the year before; the 2016–2018 files are the fixed-width public-use files, and the Census Bureau's updated processing system, used from the 2019 file on, may affect comparisons between 2017 and 2018. Food insecurity: USDA ERS (12.7%, 12.3%, 11.8%, 11.1% and 10.5% in 2015–2019; 10.2%, 12.8%, 13.5% and 13.7% in 2021–2024). Shares are of households, so the health and child-care figures differ slightly from the family-based figures above. Housing costs include utilities, so the housing and energy lines overlap in what they cover; they count as separate burdens because the thresholds are separate conventions. Transportation is excluded for lack of household-level data.

  • Starting-household budget. Two adults and a child under 5, 2015–2019 and 2021–2024. Rent: weighted median gross rent (GRNTP, including utilities) of renter households in units with two or more bedrooms that moved in within the past year (ACS 1-year), times 12. Buying variant: principal and interest on 90% of the weighted median value of mortgaged homes whose owners moved in within the past year (ACS), at the year's average Freddie Mac 30-year rate, in place of rent. Child care: weighted median annual child-care spending among CPS ASEC households with a child under 5 that pay for care (CARE_VAL, later HCHCARE_VAL). Health: KFF's average worker contribution for employer family coverage. Food and transportation: 2021 Consumer Expenditure Survey spending of consumer units headed by someone aged 25–34 ($7,942 and $11,709), moved to other years with the CPI for food and for private transportation. Income: median household income of householders aged 25–34, Census Table H-10, current dollars.

  • First-time buyer payment. ACS 1-year files, 2021–2024. Typical first home: weighted median value (VALP) of mortgaged owner homes (TEN = 1) whose householder, aged 25–44 (HHLDRAGEP), moved in within the past 12 months (MV = 1). Payment: principal and interest on 90% of that value at the year's average Freddie Mac 30-year rate, over the weighted median income (HINCP times ADJINC) of renter households with a householder aged 25–44. Actual payment: weighted median of 12 times the monthly mortgage payment (MRGP) over household income for those recent buyers. VALP is the owner's estimate of value, close to the price paid for recent buyers; the age band includes some repeat buyers.

  • Home buying rate and downgrading. ACS 1-year household files, 2015–2019 and 2021–2024. Recent buyers are owner-occupied homes with a mortgage (TEN = 1) whose householder moved in within the past 12 months (MV = 1), per 100 households in the same householder age group (HHLDRAGEP from 2021; for 2015–2019, the age of person 1 from the person file). Downgrading measures for recent buyers aged 25–44: bedrooms (BDSP), rooms (RMSP), structure type (BLD), year built, and home value (VALP) relative to the weighted median owner-occupied value in the same PUMA. Comparisons at the same income hold household income fixed in 20 bins set at the 2021 cutpoints.
